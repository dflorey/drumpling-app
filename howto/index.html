Operating manual FS-5

How to use Drumpling

A drum machine with the sounds of eight classics, from the Korg KR-55 to the E-mu SP-1200, and a real acoustic kit. Pick a style, choose how busy the groove should be and in which time signature, and count it in with an intro. Throw in fills, end with a proper ending, switch to half-time, swing it, loosen it up like a human drummer, shape it with effects, edit it on the display, and hear the same groove on another machine.

01 Quick start

  1. Pick a style with the keys at the bottom: Rock, Pop, Funk, Shuffle, Bossa, Samba, Hip-Hop or Reggae. It brings its own tempo, swing and machine.
  2. Press Start / Stop (or Space), or Intro / End to count it in with an intro. The groove plays at the style’s usual tempo.
  3. Move the Complexity slider from a very simple beat up to a sophisticated one, while it plays.
  4. Add a fill with Fill 1 or Fill 2, try the groove in Half time, in another time signature or on another machine, and finish with Intro / End, which plays the ending.

The rows stay the same at every complexity, so you see the groove grow. Blocks are as tall as the hits are loud: big ones accents, small ones ghost notes; lines mark the beats.   6. The playhead moves along the step being heard.

Editing the groove

The groove on the display is yours to change, while it plays or while it is stopped. Every complexity of every style keeps its own changes, also the next time you come back.

  • Click a step to cycle it: normal, accent, ghost note, off.
  • Drag a step up or down to set how hard it is hit: its MIDI velocity, 1 to 127, one step per pixel. The name of its row shows the velocity while you drag, and the block grows and shrinks with it. An empty step starts from 0; dragged down to 0, it is off again. A click goes on from the nearest of normal, accent and ghost.
  • The grooves are played, not just written: hi-hat, ride and shaker lean on the beat and less on the notes between, and no two hits are quite alike. On the acoustic kit each drum was recorded at several strengths, and the velocity picks the recording, so a ghost note on the snare is a real ghost note.
  • Drag a step sideways (or a row by its name) left or right to play that sound up to 50 ms early or late: a snare laid back, hi-hats pushing. Its notes move off the grid and its name turns dark, like the Edited flag; click the name to put the sound back on time. While you drag, the name shows the offset in milliseconds.
  • Drag a ▾ handle on a beat line to stretch one beat and squeeze the next (each between 50 % and 150 %; the bar keeps its length), so the bar rushes and drags. Changed beats show their length above the grid instead of their number. Double-click a handle to even it out again.
  • While stopped, click Bar 1/2 to see and edit the other bar of a two-bar groove.
  • Any change shows ↺ Edited. Click it, then Revert? again within three seconds, to go back to the groove as written: steps, timing and beats together.

03 Playing

Start / Stop

Starts the groove from its first beat, and stops it. Its LED is lit while it plays.

Intro / End

Stopped, it starts the machine with a bar of intro (I or E), then the groove from its first bar with a crash. Playing, it plays the style’s ending, in this bar or the next as a fill does, then its final hits on the next downbeat, and the machine stops. Every style has its own, at every complexity and in every time signature, simpler or busier with the level: rock counts in with the sticks and builds to marching toms, pop with handclaps and a swelling snare roll, funk with the hi-hat and kick-and-snare stabs, shuffle with the ride and triplets, bossa with the clave and congas, samba with the shaker, agogô and surdos, hip-hop with a cross-stick and a boom-bap preview, reggae with a cross-stick, a rack-a-tack and a tom roll into the drop. The LED blinks while the intro or the ending waits, then stays lit while it plays.

Fill 1 · Fill 2

A fill written for the style and complexity. Pressed in the first half of a bar it plays in that bar, later on in the next one; the bar after it starts with a crash. The LED blinks while it waits, stays lit while it plays.

Half time

Bass drum and snare stretch over twice the length, so the backbeat falls on 3, while the hi-hats and percussion keep the tempo. Press again for the normal groove.

Click

A metronome click on every beat, in 5/8, 6/8 and 7/8 on every eighth, higher on the first of the bar (M). It goes straight to the output, past the effects.

98
Tempo

40–240 BPM. Click and type a value, drag or scroll (Shift for fine steps), double-click for the style’s usual tempo. Picking a style sets its tempo.

Tap

Tap at least twice in the tempo you want (T); it follows the average of all your taps. After a pause of two seconds a new series starts.

Swing

Delays every second note: 50 % is straight, 66 % a triplet feel, 75 % at most. Shuffle and Reggae swing their eighths, the others sixteenths. Picking a style sets its swing: Shuffle 66 %, Reggae 60 %, Hip-Hop 55 %, the rest straight.

Sloppy

How human the drummer is. At 0 % every hit is on the grid, as a machine plays; 25 % is a pro, 50 % a typical band drummer, 75 % a sloppy one and 100 % a beginner. Every hit moves a little off the grid, and its strength varies more. Like a real player's, the timing doesn't jump about at random: it drifts, the whole kit rushing and dragging together over a few beats or bars, each hand and foot wobbling a little on its own, a touch ahead of the beat.

Volume

How loud the machine plays, 0–100 % (double-click: 80 %).

Knobs: drag up or down (hold Shift for fine steps), scroll, or use the arrow keys when selected. Double-click a knob to reset it.

04 Effects

The blue plate under the start keys shapes the whole kit.

Punch

On and off: a compressor squeezes the kit together, so it hits harder, the room sound comes up, and it gets a little louder.

Gated

On and off: the gated reverb of 80s records (G), a big dense burst on the snare, clap and toms that stops dead after a third of a second.

Stutter

Hold it (or R) and from the next sixteenth the slice you hear repeats in time, rolling faster: eighths for a beat, sixteenths for a beat, then thirty-seconds. Let go and the live beat is back.

Scratch

Hold it (or S) and the beat is scratched like a record under a DJ’s hand: pushed and pulled, faster and slower, forwards and backwards, in random old-school figures in time with the beat. Let go and the live beat is back.

Comp

Parallel drum bus compression: a hard-squashed copy of the kit is mixed in under the dry one, so each hit keeps its attack while the body, the room and the ghost notes come up and the kit gets louder; turned up high it pumps with the beat.

Filter

A DJ filter: off in the centre, a resonant low-pass to the left, a high-pass to the right.

Delay

Echoes on dotted eighths, in time with the tempo, louder and longer as you turn it up.

Reverb

Puts the kit in a hall, each drum as much as in a studio mix: the snare the most, clap and rim a lot, toms and percussion a good deal, cymbals and hi-hats a little, the bass drum almost dry.

05 Kits

Kit slider

Picks one of nine kits; the groove playing carries on with its sounds as soon as they have loaded. Picking a style moves it to the style’s own kit.

The nine
  • Korg KR-55 (1979): a warm analog rhythm box, made for Latin and ballroom rhythms (Bossa).
  • Roland TR-808 (1980): the booming bass drum of hip-hop, electro and dancehall (Reggae).
  • Linn LM-1 (1980): the first sampled drum machine, the sound of Prince (Shuffle).
  • Oberheim DMX (1981): punchy early hip-hop and 80s funk (Funk).
  • LinnDrum (1982): the polished sound of 80s pop (Pop).
  • Roland TR-909 (1983): the backbone of house and techno.
  • Roland TR-707 (1985): Chicago house, Italo disco and bright percussion (Samba).
  • E-mu SP-1200 (1987): gritty 12-bit golden-age hip-hop (Hip-Hop).
  • Acoustic kit: a real birch drum kit with Paiste and Stagg cymbals, the Salamander Drumkit (Rock).

Every groove plays on every kit. Where a machine lacks a sound (the TR-909 has no congas, cowbell or shaker, for instance), the nearest of its own sounds, retuned, stands in. Every kit is mixed in stereo as you would hear it from the drummer’s seat: bass drum, snare and clap in the middle, hi-hat and crash on the left, ride on the right, the toms from high (left) to floor (right), cowbell and clave left, congas and shaker right.

The Complexity slider above the style keys picks one of eight grooves for each style, from very simple (bass drum, snare and hi-hat) to sophisticated (syncopation, ghost notes, toms and percussion), each with its own two fills and ending. It changes the groove on the spot, in time.

Time signatures

Click the time signature left of the tempo (4/4) and pick 3/4, 4/4, 5/4, 7/4, 5/8, 6/8 or 7/8. Every style plays all eight of its complexities in each, with its own sounds and feel: 5/4 runs 3 + 2 with the backbeat on 3 and 5, 7/4 runs 4 + 3, 5/8 counts 3 + 2 eighths with the backbeat on the 2, 6/8 has its backbeat on 4, 7/8 counts 2 + 2 + 3. Bossa nova's rim plays the Afro-Cuban bell's figure in 5/8 and 6/8, the samba surdo lands at the end of each group, and reggae's one drop falls on each group's heavy beat. The fills and the ending fit the bar and grow busier with the complexity.

The tempo always counts quarter notes, so a 6/8 at 120 has its eighths as fast as 4/4's. The click sounds on each beat in 3/4, 4/4, 5/4 and 7/4, and on every eighth in 5/8, 6/8 and 7/8, high on the first of the bar and low on the rest. Swing stays out of 5/8, 6/8 and 7/8, whose eighths are grouped already. Your changes to a groove are kept for each style, time signature and complexity.

08 Questions

I hear nothing

Browsers only allow sound after you press a key on the page, so the first Start may take a moment. Check your volume, the Volume knob, and on iPhone the silent switch.

The display says “Loading sounds…”

The machine’s samples are downloading, half a megabyte to a megabyte. Once loaded they stay, so switching back is instant.

Where are the kit and complexity names under the sliders?

Held upright, Drumpling keeps the display large and fits everything on the screen without scrolling, so the sliders sit side by side without their name readouts: the display names the kit, and the blocks next to the style show the complexity. Turn the screen sideways to see them under the sliders.

Which settings are kept?

The machine, style, complexity, tempo, swing, volume and the effect knobs are remembered for your next visit. Half time, Punch and Gated always start off.

Can I use it offline, like an app?

Yes. Install it from your browser (“Add to Home Screen” on iPhone and iPad, “Install app” in Chrome and Edge). The machines you have played once then work without a connection.
